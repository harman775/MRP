Executive summary

What we found, and what we built

Three years of decline set the brief: alumni giving down 18%, employer partnership enquiries down 22%, event attendance down 31%. The cause was not apathy — it was that no single system held the data, so every question needed a week of spreadsheet reconciliation before anyone could act.

We consolidated the records into one MongoDB collection of 70 standardised fields, built a React front end over a FastAPI service layer, and shipped three role-specific portals: an administrator dashboard, an alumni self-service portal, and an employer recruitment portal.

The analysis changed what the university thought it knew. Engagement is not evenly spread — of 2,000 alumni account for almost every event registration and mentorship offer, and those same people give roughly times more per gift than everyone else. That single finding moved fundraising from mass mail to event-led cultivation.

Two models run on top of the data: a weighted donor propensity score and a mentor match score. Both are transparent formulas, not black boxes, so an administrator can see exactly why a name is on the list.

Section 01 · Problem

The data existed.
The answers didn't.

Career services and alumni relations both kept alumni data — in separate Excel workbooks, with different column names, updated by different people on different schedules. Nothing was missing. Everything was unreconcilable.

Organisational context

A mid-sized university under competitive pressure

Global Horizon University graduates across seven disciplines — Computer Science, Physics, Mathematics, Biology, Economics, History and English — and competes for students against institutions that publish outcome data as a matter of course. Alumni relationships drive three things GHU is measured on: reputation, fundraising, and student placement.

The operational reality behind those three was a folder of spreadsheets. Producing a single board slide on employment outcomes took the career services office several days of manual reconciliation, and the answer was stale by the time it was presented.

Problem statement

GHU has no centralised platform for tracking, analysing and acting on alumni career paths, engagement and giving. Without one, administrators cannot see outcomes in time to act, alumni have no reason to return to the institution between donation appeals, and employers cannot find graduates without going through staff by email.

Stakeholders

Three people, three different broken workflows

Administrator

Wants evidence for a decision

Needs program effectiveness, top majors, giving history and engagement in one view — for accreditation, board reporting and curriculum planning. Pain: cannot produce a visual report without a week of manual work, so decisions get made on anecdote.

Alumnus

Wants a reason to come back

Needs to manage a profile, find events, and be visible for mentorship and hiring. Pain: the only contact from the university is a donation appeal, and there is no way to see or correct what the institution holds about them.

Employer

Wants a shortlist, not a mailbox

Needs to filter graduates by major, industry, GPA and experience, then contact them. Pain: sorting hundreds of résumés with no access to a pre-qualified pool, and a hiring cycle that runs through a career services inbox.

A fourth stakeholder is easy to miss: the faculty supervisor, whose feedback on scope, citation and chart labelling shaped every deliverable in this report.

Problem validation

Three years of decline, measured

Institutional decline, three-year change

GHU institutional records, 2021–2024
Every line that depends on an alumni relationship moved the same direction at the same time. That pattern points at a systems failure, not at three separate ones.

What the evidence said

  • 18% fall in annual alumni giving over three years.
  • 22% fall in employer partnership enquiries.
  • 31% fall in event attendance.
  • 64% of recent graduates said in exit surveys that they wanted better career networking tools.

What the market said

  • Universities running an integrated alumni platform report materially higher donation rates and event attendance than those running manual systems (CASE, 2024).
  • Dashboards with real visualisation shortened administrative decision-making against static reports (DataEd Consortium, 2024).
  • Self-service portals with visible completeness indicators lift participation (Brown et al., 2023) — the finding behind our profile completeness field.
  • A survey of 150 institutions found most wanted a lighter, more modern alternative to enterprise suites (EDUCAUSE, 2024).

From problem to build

The five objectives we set

  1. Consolidate — every alumni record into one database with no loss, reconciled against source files.
  2. Analyse — six or more interactive visualisations returning in under a second, so a question can be asked and answered in a meeting.
  3. Engage — lift event registration through a self-service portal instead of email broadcast.
  4. Recruit — cut employer candidate search time with real filtering and direct contact.
  5. Predict — score donor likelihood and mentor fit with formulas an administrator can read and challenge.

Generation rules

How the synthetic data was made, and what it can be trusted for

Variable groupGeneration logicAnchored to
DemographicsSampled from national undergraduate enrolment distributions across ten statesNCES postsecondary data
AcademicGPA drawn from a correlated Gaussian, bounded 2.0–4.0; graduation years weighted to a 2024 peakJordon et al. (2022)
EmploymentPlacement probability set higher for STEM majors; recent graduates penalised for time-to-first-jobBLS labour statistics
SalaryLog-normal within major, adjusted upward by years since graduationGlassdoor / BLS wage bands
EngagementLong-tailed score with a small highly active cohort — deliberately not a normal curveCASE engagement framework
GivingGift size scales with engagement score, then the total is calibrated to the reported annual figureCASE (2022)
MentorshipInterest probability driven by engagement, with a soft-skill component in the match scoreVolunteer-likelihood modelling
ConsentRandomised grant timestamps and channels with a revocation rateGDPR retention practice

What this dataset cannot tell you

The salary figures are internally consistent but not calibrated to real market levels, and the ordering of majors by salary is a property of the generator rather than a finding about the labour market. Treat salary as a relative signal for testing the dashboard, never as an outcome claim. Everything structural — the shape of the engagement curve, the relationship between engagement and giving, the fill rate of optional fields — is what the platform was built and tested against.

02 · Model evaluation

Does the propensity score actually separate donors?

The production donor score includes past giving, so testing it against "did they give" would be circular. This evaluation uses an affinity-only model — engagement, capacity and tenure, with past giving removed — and scores it against the actual donor flag. That is the honest test of whether behaviour predicts giving.

ROC curve

True positive rate against false positive rate, all thresholds
Method — records sorted by affinity score descending; TPR and FPR accumulated at every threshold. AUC by the trapezoidal rule.

Cumulative gains

Share of donors captured against share of the base contacted
Method — cumulative donor count at each percentile of the ranked list, against the random-selection diagonal.

The lift table is the version a development office would actually use: contact the top three deciles, reach a disproportionate share of donors, and stop where lift crosses 1.0.

Cumulative Giving =
VAR CurrentVal = [Total Giving]
RETURN

The two dialects differ most on running totals: Tableau's table calculations follow the visual's addressing, while DAX needs the filter context written out explicitly. That difference is worth being able to explain — it is the single most common source of a Pareto line that looks right but is not.

The parameter that matters

Exposing the capacity weight as a parameter turned an argument into a demonstration. Set it to 40% and the donor list fills with well-paid alumni who have never attended anything; set it to 20% and the list changes substantially. Being able to show that live is more persuasive than any static chart of the same finding.

Step 5 · Publish

Delivery checklist

  1. Extract, not live connection — the source is a flat file and the aggregations are heavy; an extract makes every filter instant.
  2. Titles state findings — "Giving concentrates in three sectors", not "Giving by industry". A reader who only sees titles should still get the argument.
  3. Axes labelled with units — every axis carries its unit, and currency is formatted consistently to thousands.
  4. Tooltips carry context — record count alongside every average, so a mean built on nine people is visibly a mean built on nine people.
  5. Colour used once — one sequential palette for magnitude, one accent for the highlighted category, nothing decorative.
  6. Row-level security — if this were ever published on real data, an employer role must not see giving fields. Enforced at the data source, not by hiding a sheet.

Alumnus

Shows an alumnus their own engagement score with a plain-language recommendation and the factors behind it. Transparency is deliberate: people are being scored, so they get to see the score.

def engagement_insight(a):
    factors = {"events_attended": a.events_attended,
               "mentorship_active": 1 if a.mentorship_interest else 0,
               "profile_complete": a.profile_completeness}
    if   a.engagement_score >= 70: rec = "Strong engagement — you're in the top tier."
    elif a.engagement_score >= 50: rec = "Stay active with regular events and updates."
    else: rec = "Low engagement — re-engagement campaign recommended."
    return {"score": a.engagement_score, "factors": factors, "recommendation": rec}

Flow 02

A development director builds a campaign

  1. Analyse — opens the overview tab, reads total giving against this year's target, and sees the gap.
  2. Rank — switches to predictions; the top ten donor scores are listed with the last gift and engagement behind each.
  3. Segment — tier one (score 80+) gets a personal call from the vice-president, tier two a targeted email, tier three the newsletter.
  4. Execute — outreach runs by tier instead of by mail merge, with staff time concentrated where the score is highest.
  5. Recalculate — gifts are recorded, donation_last_year updates, and every score recomputes on the next load.

The loop matters more than any single number: the model's inputs are updated by the outcomes it produced, so next year's list is built on this year's evidence.

Section 05 · Architecture

Three tiers,
decoupled on purpose.

Presentation, application and data are separated so the analytics layer can change without touching the portals, and so the same API serves a React dashboard today and a mobile client later.

Stack decisions

Why each piece, and what it cost us

React

Component reuse across three portals that share cards, tables and filter bars, plus a mature charting ecosystem. Cost: a build step and a larger initial bundle than a server-rendered page would need.

FastAPI

Async request handling, automatic OpenAPI documentation, and Pydantic validation that made malformed writes impossible to land. Cost: a second runtime to deploy alongside the front end.

MongoDB

Alumni records are genuinely heterogeneous — a 2027 graduate has no salary and no employer. A document store absorbs that without a wall of nullable columns, and the aggregation pipeline does the analytics work close to the data. Cost: we gave up the referential guarantees a relational schema would have enforced for us.

Recharts

Declarative charts that re-render from state, which is what makes the filters feel instant. Cost: less control over fine layout than drawing SVG directly, which is why this report site draws its own.

Redis

The overview aggregations are identical for every administrator and change slowly. Caching them is the difference between a dashboard that opens and one that loads.

The campaign that tested it

Tiered outreach, run on the donor score

Conversion by donor tier

Percent of contacted alumni who gave
Conversion tracks the score almost linearly, which is the practical validation of the model. The tier-one rate is what justifies putting a vice-president on twenty phone calls instead of sending two thousand emails.

How the campaign was structured

  1. Tier 1 · score 80–100 — 20 alumni, personal calls from the vice-president.
  2. Tier 2 · score 60–79 — 50 alumni, targeted email from the development team.
  3. Tier 3 · score 40–59 — 200 alumni, newsletter inclusion, fully automated.
  4. Recalculate — gifts recorded, scores recomputed, next year's list rebuilt from this year's result.

Staff hours went where the probability was. That is the entire proposition of predictive scoring in a small development office — not more money from the same people, but the same money from fewer hours.

The misses

Two targets we did not reach, and why

Missed

Portal adoption: 16% against a 40% target

Only of 2,000 alumni cross the activity threshold. We set the target from vendor marketing rather than from GHU's own baseline, and we shipped no email or mobile prompt to bring people back. Adoption is a communications problem that a login screen cannot solve on its own.

Partly met

Engagement lift: +8% against a +10% target

Event registration rose sharply among alumni already active. It moved very little among the low-engagement majority, so the aggregate lift fell short. The platform amplified existing engagement rather than creating new engagement — a real and repeatable finding, and the argument for the re-engagement campaign in the next-steps plan.

Section 07 · Ethics & lessons

Scoring people
is not a neutral act.

The platform ranks alumni by how likely they are to give money and how suitable they are as mentors. That is useful, and it is also a form of institutional judgement applied to individuals who never asked to be ranked. Everything below follows from taking that seriously.

Privacy

Consent that can actually be withdrawn

Granular control

Alumni choose separately what is visible: employment, salary, contact details, giving history. Consent is opt-in at registration and revocable at any point, with consent_type, consent_status and granted_at stored on the record.

Deletion that means deletion

A GDPR erasure request removes personal data within thirty days. Only anonymised aggregates survive for institutional reporting, and retention_expiry makes the clock explicit rather than a policy document nobody reads.

Security

Encryption in transit and at rest, role-based access so an employer account can never reach giving data, two-factor authentication for staff, audit logging on every write, and a defined breach notification path.

This project used entirely synthetic data precisely because these protections are easy to promise and hard to build. Nothing on this site was ever a real person.

Fairness

Where the models could go wrong

The risk we can name

The donor score gives 20% of its weight to salary. Salary correlates with industry, industry correlates with major, and major correlates with the resources a student had before they arrived. Left unchecked, a fundraising list built this way quietly reproduces the advantage its subjects started with, then calls the result a prediction.

The same applies to GPA filtering in the employer portal. It is a legitimate screen and it is also a proxy for how much a student had to work during their degree.

What we do about it

  • Capped weights. Capacity is held at 20% and engagement at 30%, so behaviour outranks wealth in the score.
  • Parity testing. Score distributions are compared across demographic groups, with proxy-variable checks on the inputs.
  • Transparency. The formula is published to the people it scores; the insights tab shows an alumnus their own factors.
  • Human review. Scores rank a call list. They never make a decision on their own, and staff can override any ranking.

Our working test before shipping any scored feature: is it legal, is it fair to the person being scored, is it defensible if published in full, and would we be comfortable explaining it to the alumnus concerned? A GPA filter passes only when it is paired with context and does not silently exclude people from ever being seen.

Access

Accessibility and inclusion

WCAG 2.1

Screen reader support, keyboard navigation, alt text on every visualisation, contrast held above the minimum ratio.

Mobile

Fully functional on a phone. Many alumni have no other device, and a desktop-only portal is an exclusion decision.

No paid tier

Every feature is available to every alumnus. Visibility to employers is not something the institution should sell.

Fair postings

Keyword screening on employer messages and an equal-opportunity requirement for participating recruiters.

Lessons

What we would tell the team that starts this next semester

Scope

Build the foundation only, first

We wanted every feature at once. Splitting the work into a minimum viable product — consolidation, dashboards, models — and a later product release is what got a stable thing finished on time instead of five half-built things.

Data

Agree the standard before the import

The cleaning phase surfaced inconsistencies between imported and original spreadsheets that cost days. Incremental validation after every load, and a written field standard agreed up front, would have caught all of it.

Design

Users get lost in structures we find obvious

Early prototypes tested badly on navigation. Clearer labels, fewer steps to the same action, and consistent layouts across the three portals fixed more usability complaints than any new feature did.

Ethics

Privacy is a dial, not a switch

Granular consent forced real trade-offs between usability, transparency and compliance. There is no setting where all three are maximised; the work is choosing the balance deliberately and writing down why.

The analytical lesson

We assumed capacity predicted giving, and weighted salary at 40% in the first version of the donor model. The data said affinity predicts giving: engaged donors give several times more per gift than well-paid unengaged ones. Re-weighting the formula changed the fundraising strategy from mass appeal to event-led cultivation. The general form of the lesson — test the assumption before you embed it in a scoring function — is the one worth carrying forward.

Working method

Parallel, not sequential

Because the scope covered data migration, dashboard development, predictive modelling, privacy design and interface prototyping, each area got a single accountable owner and the work ran in parallel rather than in sequence. Daily check-ins were short and existed to surface blockers early.

The hardest week was data cleaning, when inconsistencies between the imported and original spreadsheets surfaced late. Incremental validation after every load became a rule from that point on.

The useful response to friction turned out to be procedural rather than interpersonal: written decisions, an explicit owner per area, and a willingness to revisit a choice when the evidence changed.

Almost every element went through several iterations — the dashboard structure, the scoring weights, the portal navigation. Treating iteration as the expected path rather than as rework is the habit worth keeping.
